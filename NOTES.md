#BACKGROUND

In quantum mechanics, systems are described by a wavefunction $\Psi$, which is in general complex. For time-independent ground states, $\Psi$ can always be chosen as real. The square of the amplitude of this wavefunction, $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi \Psi^*$, at any particular point gives the probability density of measuring the particle at a given position $\mathbf{R}$. 
A specific quantity of interest in quantum systems is the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ - an operator which corresponds to the total energy of the system. 
The expectation value of the energy for a system with wavefunction $\Psi$ is given by:
$$E = \frac{\langle \Psi | \hat{H} | \Psi \rangle}{\langle \Psi | \Psi \rangle} = \frac{\int d\mathbf{R}\, \Psi(\mathbf{R})^2 E_L(\mathbf{R})}{\int d\mathbf{R}\, \Psi(\mathbf{R})^2} = \int d\mathbf{R}\, \rho(\mathbf{R}) E_L(\mathbf{R})$$
where $\rho(\mathbf{R}) = \frac{\Psi(\mathbf{R})^2}{\int d\mathbf{R}\, \Psi(\mathbf{R})^2} $ is the probability density function associated with the wavefunction $\Psi$. The exact form of $\Psi$ for an arbitrary quantum system is found by solving the Shrödinger eqaution for that system, however it is general not possible to obtain analytical solutions with the exception of a select few systems such as the quantum harmonic oscillator. Various numerical methods are therefore necessary to evaluate more complex quantum systems. One such method is Variational Monte Carlo, which hinges on the fundamental variational principle of quantum mechanics. This states that the ground state energy $ E_0 $ associated with the true ground state wavefunction of the system, $\Phi_{true}$, is the lowest possible energy of the system. One can then generate a trial wavefunction $\Phi_{trial}$ and calculate the energy $E_{trial}$ associated with it, and the variational principle ensures $E_{trial} \ge E_0$, with equality iff $\Phi_{trial} = \Phi_{true} $.
